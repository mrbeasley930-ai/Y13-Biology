Session 5: Oxidative phosphorylation — Where most of the ATP is made

Everything in Lessons 3 and 4 was collecting hydrogen on NAD and FAD. This lesson spends it. The spec sentence is exact: 'synthesis of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation is associated with the transfer of electrons down the electron transfer chain and passage of protons across inner mitochondrial membranes and is catalysed by ATP synthase embedded in these membranes (chemiosmotic theory)'. It is the same mechanism as the thylakoid in Lesson 1 — so learn the comparison, because the 25-mark essay on 'the importance of membranes' (June 2023 Paper 3) lists both.

1 · The electron transfer chain and chemiosmosis

Four steps, in order, exactly as for the thylakoid: coenzymes give up hydrogen; electrons move down the chain; protons are pumped; protons return through ATP synthase. Then the fifth, mitochondria-only step: oxygen takes the electrons.

  • The mitochondrion — Label the four parts and put a process on each.
  • Coenzymes oxidised — What do reduced NAD and reduced FAD hand over, and where does it split into what?
  • Electron transfer chain — What is released at each carrier, and what is it used to move?
  • Chemiosmosis — Which compartment fills with protons? Which way do they flow through ATP synthase?
  • Oxygen — What does it accept, and what does it form? What happens to the whole chain without it?
  • NAD vs FAD — Why does reduced FAD give less ATP?

2 · Chloroplast and mitochondrion compared

The spec introduction to 3.5 says it in one sentence: 'In both respiration and photosynthesis, ATP production occurs when protons diffuse down an electrochemical gradient through molecules of the enzyme ATP synthase, embedded in the membranes of cellular organelles.' Learn the table and you have a paragraph for the membranes essay and the 'indirect evidence for evolution' point.

3 · ATP accounting and inhibitors

The spec does not demand a precise ATP total, but it does expect you to reason about yields and about what happens when a step is blocked. Modern values (about 30–32 ATP per glucose) are lower than the textbook 38 because some of the gradient is spent on transporting ADP, ATP and pyruvate.

[today’s date] The ATP account, inhibitors and uncouplers
What the specification requires:
  • The approximate ATP yield of aerobic and anaerobic respiration; the contribution of each stage
  • Applying knowledge of the chain to predict the effects of inhibitors of carriers and of substances that make the membrane leaky to protons
Read first: the ATP yield / summary section of the respiration chapter. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Where the ~32 ATP come from. Branches to explore:
  • Substrate-level — How many from glycolysis, how many from Krebs?
  • Reduced coenzymes — How many reduced NAD and reduced FAD per glucose, and from which stages?
  • Why not 38 — What else does the proton gradient pay for?
  • Inhibitor (e.g. cyanide) — What stops? What accumulates? What happens to oxygen uptake and ATP?
  • Uncoupler (e.g. DNP) — What leaks? Does electron flow stop? What happens to oxygen uptake, ATP and heat?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ An ATP account table by stage
  • ☐ A boxed anaerobic-vs-aerobic yield contrast
  • ☐ An inhibitor vs uncoupler contrast in two columns (oxygen uptake, ATP, what accumulates)

Beyond the spec · Building mitochondria

Super-curricular: not on the specification, not scored. This is the material that turns an A-level answer into a personal-statement paragraph and an interview answer.

In 1967 John Holloszy showed that endurance training roughly doubles the mitochondrial content of rat muscle; the same plasticity is now measured in people with muscle biopsies. More mitochondria, more cristae, more electron transfer chains and ATP synthase per gram of muscle: a higher rate of oxidative phosphorylation, so the athlete can run faster before relying on anaerobic glycolysis and lactate. VO2 max — the maximum rate of oxygen uptake — is in large part a measure of how fast the body can deliver oxygen to those final electron acceptors.

Nottingham's David Greenfield Human Physiology Unit does exactly this kind of work: cycle ergometers and treadmills to drive the muscle, indirect calorimetry to measure oxygen use, and Bergstrom needle biopsies to look at the mitochondria themselves. If you apply, name it.

Go and look

  • Holloway (2017) Nutrition and training influences on mitochondrial ADP sensitivity — Sports Medicine (open access)
    Reviews Holloszy's 1967 result and fifty years of mitochondrial biogenesis research.
  • Nottingham — Physiology, Metabolism and Nutrition research group
    The School of Life Sciences group behind the human physiology unit.
  • HHMI BioInteractive — Cellular Respiration Hub
    Animated ETC and ATP synthase; worth 20 minutes before the exam.

Write it down

In 60–100 words: explain why an endurance athlete's resting heart rate falls and their lactate threshold rises after six months of training. Use mitochondria, cristae, oxidative phosphorylation and final electron acceptor.
