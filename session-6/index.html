1 · Precision: what the examiner credits

Each question gives four versions of a sentence a student might write. Pick the one an AQA examiner would credit. The 'why' tells you which words carried the mark — and which lost it.

[today’s date] Precision: the words examiners credit
What the specification requires:
  • Use of correct scientific terminology (the June 2025 examiner's report calls imprecise terminology 'a perennial problem')
Read first: your own notes from Sessions 1–5 and the glossary at the back of your textbook. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Write this, not that. Branches to explore:
  • Coenzymes — Which form, which process? NAD / NADP / FAD.
  • Process names — Replace 'converted' with the real verb every time: phosphorylated, oxidised, reduced, decarboxylated, hydrolysed.
  • Energy — Released, transferred, used — never produced.
  • Anaerobic — Reduced to lactate; reduced to ethanol via ethanal — and what is NOT produced in each.
  • Oxygen — Its exact role, in five words.
  • Molecule names — GP, TP, RuBP in full and spelt right.
  • Practical words — mass/volume/concentration of…; solvent not solution; solubility.
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ A two-column 'write this / not this' table of at least ten rows, built from your own red answers in Sessions 1–5
  • ☐ A boxed rule: if a process has a name, use the name; if a molecule has a form, give the form

The mind map is a suggestion, not a template: a table, a flow diagram or numbered notes are all fine, as long as the checklist is covered. Dr Borg wants these notes to be yours, made from the textbook.

2 · Synoptic quick-fire

Twelve questions across both topics, in no particular order — the way the paper does it. The brief asks for a one-page map of both processes from your own Session 1–5 notes; make it before you start, then use it to check yourself.

[today’s date] Photosynthesis and respiration on one page
What the specification requires:
  • 3.5.1 and 3.5.2 as a whole: the two processes side by side
Read first: the summary pages of both chapters. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Energy transfers. Branches to explore:
  • Photosynthesis — LDR → LIR as one line each, with where, inputs, outputs.
  • Respiration — Four stages as one line each, with where, inputs, outputs.
  • Shared molecules — Triose phosphate, ATP, the proton gradient — where does each appear in both?
  • Opposites — Water split vs water formed; CO2 fixed vs CO2 released; NADP reduced vs NAD oxidised.
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ One page, both processes, drawn as flow lines with locations
  • ☐ A short list of what the two processes share and where they are opposite

3 · Maths and data

At least 10% of the A-level marks are maths. In this topic that means rates, ratios, percentages, standard form, significant figures, Rf, and reading a limiting-factor graph. Work each one on paper; the options are chosen so that a predictable slip lands on a wrong answer.

[today’s date] The maths of this topic
What the specification requires:
  • MS 0.1 (units), 0.2 (standard form), 1.1 (significant figures), 2.4 (rates), percentage change, ratios, Rf, inverse square law, reading limiting-factor graphs
Read first: the maths skills appendix of your textbook and the practical pages for RP7–9. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Maths you will meet. Branches to explore:
  • Rates — Which unit conversions come first? What does each word after 'per' mean you must divide by?
  • Standard form and s.f. — Write the rule for each in one line, with a wrong example and a right one.
  • Percentages and ratios — Formula for percentage change; how to express a ratio.
  • Rf and capillary volume — Formulas, with units.
  • Graphs — How to tell what is limiting from the shape.
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ Seven one-line rules with a worked example under each
  • ☐ The inverse square law stated once

The mind map is a suggestion, not a template: a table, a flow diagram or numbered notes are all fine, as long as the checklist is covered. Dr Borg wants these notes to be yours, made from the textbook.

4 · Exam questions, mark schemes and exemplars

Four questions, 20 marks: do them in 25 minutes without notes, then reveal. The first is the six-marker — eight minutes, by the clock.

[today’s date] Timing and technique for the real paper
About 1 minute per mark, including reading. For a 6-marker: 30 seconds planning (list the six ideas as single words in the margin), 6 minutes writing, 30 seconds checking every coenzyme has its form and every process has its name.
If a question gives you information (a passage, a graph, a result), the mark scheme will contain that information rewritten: use it, quote it, and then explain it with the mechanism.

Beyond the spec · Writing it into the personal statement

Super-curricular: not on the specification, not scored. This is the material that turns an A-level answer into a personal-statement paragraph and an interview answer.

A sport-science admissions tutor reads hundreds of statements that say 'I have always loved sport'. The ones that stand out say something specific about the science and show the applicant went and found out more. Over these six sessions you have four things you can write about with real understanding: the phosphocreatine–glycolysis–oxidative sequence in a race; lactate as a fuel rather than a waste product; fat-burning and glycogen sparing; mitochondrial adaptation to training. Pick two, and name the source you read.

The British Association of Sport and Exercise Sciences (BASES) runs a student conference and lists endorsed degrees; The Physiological Society's careers pages explain what the discipline is and where it leads. The Royal Society's 2024 'Science of Winning' panel, with Chrissie Wellington and cardiologist Sanjay Sharma, is 90 minutes of exactly the conversations you would have on the course.

Go and look

  • BASES — Students
    Course finder, careers guide, student conference.
  • The Physiological Society — Sport and exercise science careers
    What the discipline is, from molecule to whole body.
  • Royal Society — Lates: the science of winning (July 2024)
    Recorded panel: Chrissie Wellington, Prof Sanjay Sharma, Prof Alan Wilson.
  • Epstein, The Sports Gene (2013)
    Genes, trainability and muscle fibre type.
  • University of Nottingham — BSc Sport and Exercise Science
    The course page: reread the module list before you write.

Write it down

Write 120–150 words of personal statement: one paragraph linking something from these six sessions to sport and exercise science, naming one source you read or listened to and one thing it changed in how you think. No 'I have always loved'.
