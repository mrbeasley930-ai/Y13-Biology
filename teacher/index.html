Mark schemes for the exam questions

Q1 (5 marks). Describe how ATP is produced in the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis.

  1. Chlorophyll absorbs light / photoionisation of chlorophyll — electrons excited / released;
  2. Electrons pass along the electron transfer chain (of carriers) in the thylakoid membrane;
  3. Energy released (as electrons pass along the chain) is used to pump / actively transport protons (H+) into the thylakoid space;
  4. Protons move down their concentration gradient / diffuse (back into the stroma) through ATP synthase;
  5. ADP + Pi → ATP (catalysed by ATP synthase) / photophosphorylation / chemiosmosis;

Five marks = the five steps in order. The commonest dropped mark is the third: candidates say “protons move across the membrane” without saying the energy from the electrons PUMPS them. Reject ‘energy is produced’.

Q2 (3 marks). Explain the role of the photolysis of water in the light-dependent reaction.

  1. Light (energy) splits water into protons, electrons and oxygen;
  2. Electrons replace those lost from chlorophyll (during photoionisation);
  3. Protons (and electrons) are used to reduce NADP / form reduced NADP; (accept: oxygen released as a by-product / used in respiration as an alternative third point)

‘Explain the role’ means say what each product is FOR. Candidates who only write the equation get one mark.

Q3 (4 marks). A scientist isolated thylakoid membranes and placed them in a solution at pH 4 so that the thylakoid space became acidic. She then rapidly transferred them to a solution at pH 8 containing ADP and phosphate. ATP was produced in the dark. Explain these results.

  1. (At pH 4) protons / H+ diffuse into the thylakoid space / thylakoid space has a high concentration of H+;
  2. (Transfer to pH 8) creates a proton / H+ (concentration) gradient across the thylakoid membrane (higher inside);
  3. Protons diffuse out (into the solution) through ATP synthase;
  4. This provides the energy to combine ADP and Pi / phosphorylate ADP, so no light / electron transfer chain is needed;

This is Jagendorf’s 1966 experiment — the evidence for chemiosmosis. The skill is recognising that the ACID BATH did the job light normally does (building the gradient). Suggest-style questions like this are where A* candidates pull ahead: state the familiar mechanism, then show which step the new situation replaces.

Sport-science task set

In 60–100 words: a 400 m runner finishes in 45 s. Using this lesson and the links, say which ATP-supplying processes are running during the race and in what order they dominate. Keep the phrase 'ATP is the immediate energy source' in it.

Mark schemes for the exam questions

Q1 (6 marks). Describe the light-independent reaction of photosynthesis.

  1. Carbon dioxide combines / reacts with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP);
  2. Produces two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP), using the enzyme rubisco;
  3. GP is reduced to triose phosphate;
  4. using reduced NADP;
  5. using energy from (the hydrolysis of) ATP;
  6. Triose phosphate is converted to glucose / hexose / RuBP / named organic substance;

This is the June 2022 Paper 2 Q1.2 mark scheme. Examiner notes: accept 'fixation' for 'reacts'; 'glucose 3-phosphate' for GP is penalised; 'TP' only scores if 'triose phosphate' is written somewhere; reduced NAD is rejected for mark 4; marks 4 and 5 must be in the context of GP → TP.

Q2 (4 marks). Scientists measured the concentrations of GP and RuBP in a culture of algae in bright light. They then switched off the light. In the first minute of darkness, the concentration of GP rose and the concentration of RuBP fell. Explain these results.

  1. In the dark the light-dependent reaction stops, so no ATP and no reduced NADP are produced;
  2. GP cannot be reduced to triose phosphate (which needs ATP and reduced NADP), so GP accumulates / rises;
  3. RuBP is still being converted to GP (CO2 still being fixed) so RuBP is used up;
  4. RuBP is not regenerated because there is no triose phosphate / no ATP (for regeneration), so RuBP falls;

Both halves of the answer must be explained with mechanism. A one-line answer ('the cycle stops') scores nothing. Think of each molecule as a tank with an inflow and an outflow and say which tap was turned off.

Q3 (4 marks). A tomato grower compared yields in two identical glasshouses over one winter. Glasshouse A was unheated. In glasshouse B she burned propane, raising the temperature from 12 °C to 20 °C and the CO2 concentration from 0.04% to 0.09%. Yield in B was 35% higher. Evaluate whether the grower should conclude that raising the CO2 concentration caused the increase in yield.

  1. Higher CO2 could have increased the rate of photosynthesis / CO2 may have been a limiting factor (in winter light);
  2. BUT temperature also rose / two variables changed, so the effect cannot be attributed to CO2 alone (confounding variable);
  3. Higher temperature would increase the rate of the (enzyme-controlled) light-independent reaction;
  4. Valid conclusion would need a glasshouse with raised CO2 only (or raised temperature only) / repeats / more than one winter;

'Evaluate' = argue both ways and reach a verdict. Mark schemes for evaluation nearly always carry one mark for the confounding variable and one for what a better design would be.

Sport-science task set

In 60–100 words: explain to a marathon runner why 'carb loading' works, using the words glycogen, glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and respiration, and say which muscle fibre type benefits most.

Mark schemes for the exam questions

Q1 (5 marks). Describe how glycolysis produces ATP and reduced NAD from glucose.

  1. (In the cytoplasm) glucose is phosphorylated using (phosphate from) ATP / 2 ATP used;
  2. to form glucose phosphate / hexose bisphosphate (more reactive);
  3. which splits into two (molecules of) triose phosphate;
  4. Triose phosphate is oxidised to pyruvate / hydrogen removed from triose phosphate, reducing NAD (to reduced NAD);
  5. ATP is formed by substrate-level phosphorylation / 4 ATP made, net gain of 2 ATP;

Say 'oxidised' and 'reduced NAD' in the same sentence — the hydrogen removed from triose phosphate is what reduces NAD. Giving the numbers (2 used, 4 made, net 2) is the cheapest mark on the paper.

Q2 (4 marks). A scientist added an inhibitor of the electron transfer chain to a culture of yeast respiring aerobically. Afterwards, the oxygen uptake of the yeast decreased, ATP production decreased, and ethanol was produced. Explain each of these changes.

  1. Oxygen uptake decreased: (less/no) oxygen required because oxygen is the final electron acceptor (at the end of the electron transfer chain);
  2. ATP production decreased: less/no oxidative phosphorylation / no proton gradient / no chemiosmosis (only glycolysis ATP remains);
  3. Ethanol produced: anaerobic respiration occurs / pyruvate is reduced (to ethanol) by reduced NAD;
  4. so that NAD is regenerated / re-oxidised, allowing glycolysis to continue;

June 2025 Q1.3 was 'one of the most effective discriminators on the paper'. Not credited: 'oxygen uptake fell because anaerobic respiration took place' (that is the result, not the cause); 'pyruvate is oxidised to ethanol'; adding lactate to the yeast answer; referring to photolysis.

Q3 (5 marks). A student used a respirometer to compare the rate of respiration of yeast at 20 °C and 30 °C. She measured the distance the coloured liquid moved in 10 minutes. Describe how she should calculate the rate of oxygen uptake in cm3 g−1 min−1, and explain two variables, other than temperature, that she must control for the comparison to be valid.

  1. Measure the diameter/radius of the capillary bore and calculate the volume moved (πr2 × distance);
  2. Divide the volume by the mass of yeast and by the time (10 min);
  3. (Subtract any movement in the control tube with glass beads;)
  4. Control variable 1 (any of): mass/concentration/volume of yeast; volume/concentration of glucose; pH; species/strain of yeast; tube size — with reason (e.g. more yeast would use more oxygen regardless of temperature);
  5. Control variable 2 (another from the list) with reason;

'Explain two variables' means name each AND say why it would otherwise distort the comparison. 'Amount of yeast' is not accepted — say mass, volume or concentration.

Mark schemes for the exam questions

Q1 (2 marks). The equation for the aerobic respiration of glucose is C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O. Six molecules of carbon dioxide are produced from each molecule of glucose. Use your knowledge of the link reaction and the Krebs cycle to explain why.

  1. One carbon dioxide from the link reaction and two carbon dioxide from the Krebs cycle (per pyruvate / per turn);
  2. Link reaction and Krebs cycle occur twice per glucose / two pyruvate per glucose; (ALTERNATIVE pair: two CO2 from the link reactions overall AND four CO2 from the Krebs cycles overall)

June 2025 Paper 2 Q1.2. 'Decarboxylation' is accepted for the release of CO2. About half of candidates got both marks; the errors were 'carboxylation', Calvin-cycle products, and 'six cycles'.

Q2 (6 marks). Describe the link reaction and the Krebs cycle.

  1. Pyruvate is oxidised / decarboxylated (in the link reaction) to acetate, producing reduced NAD (and carbon dioxide);
  2. Acetate combines with coenzyme A to form acetylcoenzyme A;
  3. Acetyl coenzyme A combines with a four-carbon molecule to form a six-carbon molecule (releasing coenzyme A);
  4. Series of oxidation–reduction / redox reactions; carbon dioxide is lost / decarboxylation;
  5. Reduced coenzymes / reduced NAD and reduced FAD are produced;
  6. ATP is produced by substrate-level phosphorylation; (accept: four-carbon molecule regenerated)

Six marks = the six bold ideas in Notes 7 and 8. Write the names in full once (acetylcoenzyme A, substrate-level phosphorylation). Location (matrix) is a good first sentence but not a mark point here.

Q3 (3 marks). During prolonged exercise, muscle cells increase the number of CD36 transport proteins in their mitochondrial membranes. CD36 transports fatty acids. Suggest and explain how this is beneficial to the muscle cells during exercise.

  1. More fatty acids enter the mitochondria (by facilitated diffusion);
  2. Fatty acids are broken down to acetyl coenzyme A / used in the Krebs cycle;
  3. (More) reduced NAD/FAD → more ATP by oxidative phosphorylation / respiration provides more ATP (for contraction); (accept: spares glucose/glycogen so it lasts longer)

Adapted from June 2023 Paper 2 Q10.4. 'Suggest' questions reward you for stating the familiar pathway (fatty acids → acetyl CoA → Krebs) and then the consequence (ATP). Do not say fatty acids enter glycolysis.

Mark schemes for the exam questions

Q1 (5 marks). Describe how ATP is produced by oxidative phosphorylation in a mitochondrion.

  1. Reduced NAD / reduced FAD is oxidised, releasing hydrogen / electrons (and protons);
  2. Electrons pass along the electron transfer chain / series of carriers (in the inner membrane), releasing energy;
  3. Energy is used to pump / actively transport protons into the inter-membrane space (creating a proton / electrochemical gradient);
  4. Protons diffuse (back into the matrix) down the gradient through ATP synthase, which forms ATP from ADP and Pi (chemiosmosis);
  5. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor (forming water);

Mark 5 is free if you remember oxygen. The commonest lost mark is 3: candidates say 'protons move into the inter-membrane space' without saying what pumps them.

Q2 (5 marks). Compare and contrast the production of ATP in the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis and in oxidative phosphorylation.

  1. Similarity: both involve electrons passing along an electron transfer chain (in a membrane) / both use ATP synthase;
  2. Similarity: both pump protons across a membrane, creating a gradient, and protons diffuse back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis);
  3. Difference: source of electrons — chlorophyll (excited by light / photoionisation) and water, vs reduced NAD / reduced FAD;
  4. Difference: location — thylakoid membrane vs inner mitochondrial membrane (protons into thylakoid space vs inter-membrane space);
  5. Difference: final electron acceptor — NADP vs oxygen (forming reduced NADP vs water);

'Compare and contrast' = at least one similarity AND at least one difference, each stated for both organelles. Write it as a table if you like; examiners accept it.

Q3 (4 marks). Scientists measured the rate of oxygen uptake and the rate of ATP synthesis of isolated mitochondria supplied with pyruvate. They then added DNP, a substance that allows protons to pass freely through the inner mitochondrial membrane. After adding DNP, the rate of oxygen uptake increased and the rate of ATP synthesis fell to almost zero. Explain these results.

  1. DNP allows protons to leak back into the matrix without passing through ATP synthase / the proton gradient is not maintained / collapses;
  2. So (little or) no ATP is made by chemiosmosis / ATP synthase has no proton flow to drive it;
  3. Electrons still pass along the electron transfer chain (so oxygen is still used as the final electron acceptor);
  4. Oxygen uptake increases because the chain runs faster / is no longer limited by the back-pressure of the gradient / more reduced NAD is oxidised; (accept: energy released as heat)

The classic uncoupler question. The key move is separating two things students fuse together: electron flow (which continues) and ATP synthesis (which needs the gradient). Say explicitly that the two are 'uncoupled'.

Sport-science task set

In 60–100 words: explain why an endurance athlete's resting heart rate falls and their lactate threshold rises after six months of training. Use mitochondria, cristae, oxidative phosphorylation and final electron acceptor.

Mark schemes for the exam questions

Q1 (6 marks). Describe the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis.

  1. Chlorophyll absorbs light / photoionisation of chlorophyll: electrons excited and released;
  2. Electrons pass along an electron transfer chain (in the thylakoid membrane), releasing energy;
  3. Energy used to pump protons (H+) into the thylakoid space / across the thylakoid membrane, creating a proton gradient;
  4. Protons diffuse through ATP synthase (into the stroma), producing ATP from ADP + Pi (photophosphorylation / chemiosmosis);
  5. Electrons (and protons) reduce NADP to form reduced NADP;
  6. Photolysis of water produces protons, electrons (which replace those lost by chlorophyll) and oxygen;

Six marks, six numbered steps, same order as Notes 2. Write 'thylakoid' at least once. The most-dropped mark is NADP reduction — candidates stop at ATP.

Q2 (5 marks). Muscle cells contain many mitochondria with densely folded inner membranes. During a 100 m sprint, muscle cells respire mainly anaerobically; during a marathon, mainly aerobically. Use your knowledge of respiration to explain why the number of mitochondria matters more for the marathon runner than for the sprinter.

  1. Anaerobic respiration (glycolysis + reduction of pyruvate to lactate) occurs in the cytoplasm, so does not need mitochondria;
  2. Anaerobic respiration gives only 2 ATP per glucose but very rapidly (sufficient for ~10 s);
  3. Aerobic respiration (link reaction, Krebs cycle, oxidative phosphorylation) occurs in the mitochondria;
  4. Oxidative phosphorylation on the inner membrane / cristae provides most of the ATP (~30 per glucose) — more mitochondria / more inner membrane = more electron transfer chains and ATP synthase = higher rate of ATP production;
  5. Marathon running requires sustained ATP production over a long time (and uses fatty acids, which can only be respired aerobically / in mitochondria);

A synoptic 'use your knowledge' question. Structure: what the sprinter uses and where; what the marathon runner uses and where; why the mitochondrial membranes matter for the second; a link to duration or substrate.

Q3 (5 marks). A student wanted to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of the light-dependent reaction using isolated chloroplasts and DCPIP. Describe a method she could use, including how she would make the results valid.

  1. Isolate chloroplasts in cold, buffered, isotonic medium (and keep on ice);
  2. Add equal volumes of chloroplast suspension to DCPIP in tubes at a range of temperatures (water baths, e.g. 10–50 °C) / equilibrate before mixing;
  3. Same light intensity for all tubes (same lamp distance) / same volume and concentration of DCPIP / same concentration of chloroplasts;
  4. Measure time for DCPIP to decolourise (rate = 1/time) OR absorbance with a colorimeter at set intervals (use a colour standard / colorimeter to make the endpoint objective);
  5. Controls: tube with no chloroplasts, tube in the dark, boiled chloroplasts; repeat at each temperature and calculate a mean;

A method question is a list of actions with the reasons that make them valid. Say the independent variable and its range, the control variables, how the dependent variable is measured objectively, and the controls plus repeats.

Q4 (4 marks). Scientists measured the rate of oxygen consumption of a cyclist at increasing exercise intensities, and the concentration of lactate in his blood. Oxygen consumption rose steadily with intensity until it levelled off at 4.2 dm3 min−1. Blood lactate stayed at about 1 mmol dm−3 up to a moderate intensity, then rose sharply as intensity increased further. Explain these results.

  1. At low/moderate intensity, ATP is supplied by aerobic respiration, so oxygen consumption rises with demand (oxygen is the final electron acceptor);
  2. Lactate stays low because pyruvate enters the mitochondria / the link reaction and Krebs cycle (and any lactate formed is removed / oxidised as fast as it is made);
  3. Oxygen consumption levels off because the maximum rate of oxygen delivery / uptake / use by mitochondria is reached (VO2 max);
  4. Above this, extra ATP comes from anaerobic respiration: pyruvate is reduced to lactate (to regenerate NAD so glycolysis continues), so lactate accumulates (the lactate threshold);

Data questions want the mechanism attached to each feature of the data: 'rises steadily because…', 'levels off because…', 'rises sharply because…'. Quote the figures.
