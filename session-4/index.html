Session 4: The link reaction and the Krebs cycle — Inside the matrix

With oxygen available, pyruvate goes into the mitochondrion and is taken apart completely. The link reaction and the Krebs cycle release all six carbons of glucose as CO2 and load hydrogen onto NAD and FAD for Lesson 5. The spec's phrase is 'in such detail as to show', and June 2025's ‘why six CO2 per glucose’ (Q1.2) is the model of how it is tested: can you count?

The examiner's report for that question says weaker candidates 'confused the Krebs cycle with the Calvin cycle'. Keep Lesson 2 and Lesson 4 in separate parts of your file.

1 · Pyruvate into the matrix and the link reaction

Three spec facts: pyruvate enters the matrix by active transport; it is oxidised to acetate, producing reduced NAD (and CO2); acetate combines with coenzyme A to form acetylcoenzyme A. Paper 3 in June 2023 asked for exactly these names in a table.

2 · The Krebs cycle

The spec wants: acetyl CoA reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A and producing a six-carbon molecule; a series of oxidation–reduction reactions generates reduced coenzymes and ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, and CO2 is lost. Names like citrate and oxaloacetate are useful but not required.

[today’s date] The Krebs cycle
What the specification requires:
  • Acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A and producing a six-carbon molecule that enters the Krebs cycle
  • In a series of oxidation–reduction reactions the Krebs cycle generates reduced coenzymes and ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, and carbon dioxide is lost
Read first: the Krebs cycle section of the respiration chapter. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Krebs cycle (matrix). Branches to explore:
  • In — 2C + 4C → ? Where does coenzyme A go?
  • Round the cycle — How many decarboxylations? How many dehydrogenations? Which two coenzymes are reduced, and how many of each?
  • ATP — How much, and by which kind of phosphorylation?
  • Out — What is regenerated to keep the cycle turning?
  • Counting — Per turn and per glucose: CO2, reduced NAD, reduced FAD, ATP. Why six CO2 per glucose overall?
  • Not the Calvin cycle — Where, what is oxidised/reduced, which coenzyme — set the two cycles side by side.

  • ☐ A per-turn / per-glucose table
  • ☐ A boxed count explaining six CO2 per glucose
  • ☐ A short Krebs-vs-Calvin contrast

Beyond the spec · Burning fat, sparing glycogen

Super-curricular: not on the specification, not scored. This is the material that turns an A-level answer into a personal-statement paragraph and an interview answer.

A trained endurance athlete at a steady pace runs on a mixture of fat and carbohydrate, and training shifts the mix towards fat at any given speed. The biochemistry is this lesson: more mitochondria, more fatty-acid transporters (CD36), more enzymes for β-oxidation and the Krebs cycle — so the limited glycogen store (about 400–500 g) is spared for the finish. The marathon 'wall' at about 30 km is glycogen running out and the body being forced onto fat alone, which cannot supply ATP fast enough for race pace.

Tim Noakes's Lore of Running has the fullest sixth-form-accessible treatment of this, and Alex Hutchinson's Endure asks the question a sport scientist is paid to answer: is the limit in the muscle, the heart, or the brain?

Go and look

  • Noakes, Lore of Running (4th ed., Human Kinetics 2003)
    Part I 'Physiology and Biochemistry of Running': energy systems, fat vs carbohydrate, the central governor.
  • Hutchinson, Endure (2018) — Kirkus review
    What actually limits endurance; readable and well-referenced.
  • HHMI BioInteractive — Cellular Respiration Hub
    Free interactive with molecular animations of the link reaction, Krebs cycle and ETC.

Write it down

In 60–100 words: explain the marathon 'wall' using the words glycogen, fatty acids, acetyl coenzyme A, Krebs cycle and rate of ATP production.
