Session 1: ATP and the light-dependent reaction — Where the energy comes from

Life depends on continuous transfers of energy — that is the first sentence of spec section 3.5, and it is the thread through all six of these sessions. Photosynthesis traps light energy in ATP and reduced NADP and then in glucose; respiration releases it again as ATP. The same machine — an electron transfer chain, a proton gradient and ATP synthase (chemiosmosis) — makes ATP in both. Learn it once here, in the chloroplast, and you will recognise it in the mitochondrion in Lesson 5.

This session has three topics to make your own notes on from the textbook, each followed by quick-check questions, then an exam section with mark schemes and marked exemplar answers, then a sport-science panel.

1 · ATP — the immediate energy source

You met ATP in Year 1 (3.1.6). Section 3.5 assumes it, so it goes in first. Every mark scheme in this topic that says “ATP” is really asking whether you know what ATP is and why cells use it.

  • Chloroplast structure (3.2.1): thylakoids, grana, stroma and their roles
Read first: your textbook's section on ATP (Year 1, biological molecules/energy) and the chloroplast section in cell structure. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: ATP. Branches to explore:
  • Structure — What three parts make it a phosphorylated nucleotide? Which bond is broken when energy is released?
  • Hydrolysis and synthesis — Write both equations with their enzymes. Which one is a condensation reaction?
  • Why ATP, not glucose? — List four reasons. Which one is about heat, which about speed?
  • Three ways cells phosphorylate ADP — Photophosphorylation, substrate-level, oxidative: where does each happen?
  • The chloroplast — Which part holds the pigments and the electron transfer chain? Which part holds the Calvin-cycle enzymes?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ A labelled diagram of ATP
  • ☐ Both equations (hydrolysis and synthesis) with enzymes named
  • ☐ A four-row table: why ATP is the immediate energy source
  • ☐ A labelled chloroplast with the two reactions placed on it
  • ☐ A boxed one-line definition of phosphorylation

2 · The light-dependent reaction

The spec asks for the light-dependent reaction “in such detail as to show” four things: photoionisation, ATP and reduced NADP as the products, chemiosmosis, and photolysis. Those four are the four mark points of any LDR question. Build your notes around them.

[today’s date] The light-dependent reaction
What the specification requires:
  • Photoionisation of chlorophyll
  • Energy from the released electrons conserved as ATP and reduced NADP
  • ATP production: electron transfer chain, protons pumped across the thylakoid membrane, ATP synthase — the chemiosmotic theory
  • Photolysis of water produces protons, electrons and oxygen
Read first: the light-dependent reaction section of the photosynthesis chapter. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Light-dependent reaction (thylakoid membrane). Branches to explore:
  • Photoionisation — What does light do to chlorophyll's electrons? What is chlorophyll left as?
  • Electron transfer chain — What happens to the electrons' energy at each carrier? What is that energy used to move, and where to?
  • Chemiosmosis — Which way do protons flow through ATP synthase? What is the ATP-making step called?
  • Reduced NADP — What does NADP accept, and from where?
  • Photolysis — Write the equation. Where does each of the three products go?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ The four steps as a numbered list, in order
  • ☐ A diagram of the thylakoid membrane: stroma one side, thylakoid space the other, protons pumped in and flowing back through ATP synthase
  • ☐ A table: product (ATP, reduced NADP, O2) — made by — used in
  • ☐ A boxed one-sentence definition of chemiosmosis

3 · Required practical 7 — chromatography of leaf pigments

RP7 is examined as method (June 2023 Paper 2 Q1.1, 4 marks) and as reasoning about solubility (Q1.2, 2 marks). It also carries the maths of Rf. Know the method as a numbered list and the one idea underneath it: pigments separate because they have different solubilities in the solvent.

[today’s date] Required practical 7 — chromatography of leaf pigments
What the specification requires:
  • Use of chromatography to investigate the pigments isolated from leaves of different plants (e.g. shade-tolerant and shade-intolerant)
  • Practical skills: Rf values; why pigments separate; evaluating the method
Read first: the required practical 7 pages (often a boxed practical in the photosynthesis chapter). Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Pigment chromatography. Branches to explore:
  • Method — Why pencil for the origin? Why must the solvent start below it? Why mark the solvent front at once?
  • Why pigments separate — Which property of a pigment decides how far it travels? Why can a different solvent separate a different number of pigments?
  • Rf — Write the formula. Can it ever exceed 1? Does it have units?
  • The pigments — Which is usually furthest up the paper, which lowest? What colour is each?
  • Linking to habitat — Why might a shade leaf carry more accessory pigment?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ A numbered six-step method
  • ☐ A boxed Rf formula with one worked example
  • ☐ One sentence on why pigments separate, in words an examiner would credit
  • ☐ A table of the four main pigments, their colours and approximate Rf values from your textbook

[today’s date] How AQA marks these questions
Describe = say what happens, in order, with the correct names. Explain = give the reason (because / so that / this means). Suggest = apply what you know to a new situation; you will not have seen it before.
Each mark is a mark point: a specific idea in specific words. Six marks = six separate ideas, not one idea written six ways.
Precision words that score: photoionisation, photolysis, electron transfer chain, proton gradient, ATP synthase, chemiosmosis, reduced NADP.
Words that lose marks: “energy is made” (energy is transferred); “NAD” in photosynthesis; “converted” where the process has a name (reduced, phosphorylated, oxidised).

Beyond the spec · ATP in the first ten seconds of a race

Super-curricular: not on the specification, not scored. This is the material that turns an A-level answer into a personal-statement paragraph and an interview answer.

A 100 m sprinter's muscles use ATP faster than any process in this lesson can make it. For the first few seconds the ATP is regenerated by phosphocreatine (PCr + ADP → creatine + ATP), then by anaerobic glycolysis (Lesson 3), and only later by the oxidative machinery you will meet in Lesson 5. Gastin's review (2001) found the aerobic and anaerobic systems contribute equally at about 75 seconds of maximal exercise — much earlier than the old textbook figure. The OpenStax physiology text puts the phosphocreatine phase at roughly the first 15 seconds of contraction.

This is why Nottingham's Sport and Exercise Science degree carries a module called Physiology, Nutrition and Metabolism in Exercise: the question “where does the ATP come from, and how fast?” is the question of the whole discipline. Entry is AAA–AAB with at least two from biology, chemistry, maths, physics, psychology or PE.

Go and look

  • University of Nottingham — BSc Sport and Exercise Science
    Official course page: entry requirements, modules including Physiology, Nutrition and Metabolism in Exercise.
  • OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, 10.3 — Muscle fiber contraction (sources of ATP)
    Free textbook: creatine phosphate, glycolysis and aerobic respiration as the three ATP sources in muscle.
  • Gastin (2001) Energy system interaction and relative contribution during maximal exercise — Sports Medicine
    The review behind the '75 seconds' figure; abstract is free.

Write it down

In 60–100 words: a 400 m runner finishes in 45 s. Using this lesson and the links, say which ATP-supplying processes are running during the race and in what order they dominate. Keep the phrase 'ATP is the immediate energy source' in it.
