Session 2: The light-independent reaction and limiting factors — Turning CO2 into sugar

The light-independent reaction (LIR) uses the ATP and reduced NADP from Lesson 1 to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. It runs in the stroma, it is a cycle, and it has been examined as a straight 6-mark “describe” (June 2022 Paper 2 Q1.2). The second half of the lesson is the applied side: what limits the rate, and how growers overcome it — the spec says you must be able to evaluate data on agricultural practices.

1 · The Calvin cycle

Four spec bullets, four mark points, plus two things the spec says you should be able to do with them (reduced NADP is used to make a sugar; ATP hydrolysis provides the extra energy). Draw it as a cycle with three stations: fixation, reduction, regeneration.

[today’s date] The light-independent reaction (Calvin cycle)
What the specification requires:
  • CO2 reacts with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP), catalysed by rubisco
  • ATP and reduced NADP from the LDR reduce GP to triose phosphate
  • Some triose phosphate regenerates RuBP; some is converted to useful organic substances
Read first: the light-independent reaction / Calvin cycle section of the photosynthesis chapter. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Calvin cycle (stroma). Branches to explore:
  • Carbon fixation — What reacts with what, catalysed by which enzyme, to make how many of what?
  • Reduction — Which molecule is reduced to which? What supplies the hydrogen, and what supplies the extra energy?
  • Regeneration — What fraction of the triose phosphate goes back round? What does that step cost?
  • The product — What can the triose phosphate that leaves become? (sugar, lipid, amino acid?)
  • Counting — How many turns per glucose? How many ATP and reduced NADP per glucose?
  • In the dark — What runs out, and how quickly? What happens to GP and RuBP levels when the light goes off, or when CO2 is removed?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ The cycle drawn as a triangle (RuBP, GP, TP) with everything that enters and leaves each side
  • ☐ The three full names written out once: ribulose bisphosphate, glycerate 3-phosphate, triose phosphate
  • ☐ A per-glucose table: turns, CO2, ATP, reduced NADP
  • ☐ A note on what happens to GP and RuBP levels in the dark and without CO2

The mind map is a suggestion, not a template: a table, a flow diagram or numbered notes are all fine, as long as the checklist is covered. Dr Borg wants these notes to be yours, made from the textbook.

2 · Limiting factors and farming

The rate of photosynthesis is set by whichever factor is in shortest supply. You need to identify limiting factors from graphs and evaluate data on how growers overcome them — which always comes down to cost versus yield.

[today’s date] Limiting factors and agricultural practices
What the specification requires:
  • Identify environmental factors that limit the rate of photosynthesis
  • Evaluate data relating to common agricultural practices used to overcome the effect of these limiting factors
Read first: the limiting factors section of the photosynthesis chapter (and the glasshouse/commercial growing examples). Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: Rate of photosynthesis. Branches to explore:
  • Light intensity — Which reaction does it drive directly? What does the graph look like, and why does it plateau?
  • CO2 concentration — Why is atmospheric CO2 usually limiting in bright light? What happens to the plateau when CO2 is raised?
  • Temperature — Which reaction is enzyme-controlled? Why does temperature barely matter at low light?
  • Reading the graph — On the rising part, what is limiting? On the plateau?
  • Glasshouses — List the practices. What does burning propane do twice over?
  • Evaluating — What four questions do you ask of yield data? What is a confounding variable?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ A definition of a limiting factor
  • ☐ A sketch graph: rate against light intensity at two CO2 concentrations, annotated with what limits where
  • ☐ A three-row table of the factors and why each limits
  • ☐ A boxed evaluation checklist for farming data

The mind map is a suggestion, not a template: a table, a flow diagram or numbered notes are all fine, as long as the checklist is covered. Dr Borg wants these notes to be yours, made from the textbook.

3 · Required practical 8 — dehydrogenase activity in chloroplasts

RP8 investigates the effect of a named factor (usually light intensity) on the rate of dehydrogenase activity in isolated chloroplasts, using the redox dye DCPIP, which is blue when oxidised and colourless when reduced. It is the practical that proves the LDR produces reducing power.

[today’s date] Required practical 8 — dehydrogenase activity in chloroplast extracts
What the specification requires:
  • Investigation into the effect of a named factor on the rate of dehydrogenase activity in extracts of chloroplasts (the Hill reaction, with DCPIP)
  • Practical skills: controls, rate from time, objective endpoints
Read first: the required practical 8 pages in the photosynthesis chapter. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: DCPIP and the Hill reaction. Branches to explore:
  • The idea — What does DCPIP accept in place of NADP? What colour change shows it has happened?
  • Isolating chloroplasts — Why cold? Why isotonic? Why buffered?
  • The variable — If it is light intensity, how is it changed? What is the inverse square law?
  • Measuring — Time to decolourise, or absorbance? Which is objective, and why?
  • Controls — Three control tubes — what does each one rule out?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ A numbered method with a reason beside each step
  • ☐ A boxed line: rate = 1/time (or change in absorbance per minute)
  • ☐ A list of the three controls and what each shows
  • ☐ A short evaluation paragraph: subjective endpoint, lamp heat, distance as a proxy for intensity

The mind map is a suggestion, not a template: a table, a flow diagram or numbered notes are all fine, as long as the checklist is covered. Dr Borg wants these notes to be yours, made from the textbook.

Beyond the spec · From the leaf to the start line

Super-curricular: not on the specification, not scored. This is the material that turns an A-level answer into a personal-statement paragraph and an interview answer.

Every gram of glycogen in an athlete's muscles was once triose phosphate in a leaf. Endurance athletes 'carbohydrate load' in the days before a race to raise muscle glycogen; the June 2023 Paper 2 mark scheme gives the chain explicitly: carbohydrate → stored as glycogen (glycogenesis) → hydrolysed to glucose (glycogenolysis) → glucose for respiration/glycolysis. Slow fibres draw on it in long events, fast fibres in short ones.

The free Coursera course Science of Exercise (University of Colorado Boulder) opens with exactly this: how carbohydrate, fat and protein become ATP at different intensities. Audit it free; its first module is a good weekend's work and a strong personal-statement line.

Go and look

  • Coursera — Science of Exercise (University of Colorado Boulder)
    Free to audit; Module 1 'The Energetics of Exercise' covers carbohydrate, fat and protein metabolism and ATP.
  • OpenLearn — Exploring sport online: athletes and efficient hearts
    Open University free 5-hour course on oxygen delivery in athletes.

Write it down

In 60–100 words: explain to a marathon runner why 'carb loading' works, using the words glycogen, glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and respiration, and say which muscle fibre type benefits most.
