Session 3: Glycolysis and anaerobic respiration — Taking glucose apart

Respiration produces ATP. Glycolysis is the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration, it happens in the cytoplasm, and it needs no oxygen. In June 2025 the gap-fill on exactly this (Paper 2 Q1.1) was answered 'mitochondrion' by the largest group of wrong candidates and 'NADP' almost whenever the coenzyme was wrong. Those two slips are what this lesson is built to prevent.

For a future sport scientist this is the lesson that matters most: lactate, the anaerobic 'debt', and why your legs burn in the last 100 m of a 400 m.

2 · Anaerobic respiration

If oxygen is absent the mitochondrial stages cannot run, so reduced NAD cannot be re-oxidised there. Unless NAD is regenerated, glycolysis stops within seconds. Anaerobic respiration is the cell's workaround — and the only step that occurs in anaerobic but not aerobic respiration is the reduction of pyruvate (June 2022 Paper 2 Q4.1).

[today’s date] Anaerobic respiration
What the specification requires:
  • If respiration is only anaerobic, pyruvate is converted to ethanol or lactate using reduced NAD; the oxidised NAD produced can be used in further glycolysis
Read first: the anaerobic respiration section of the respiration chapter. Make the notes in your own words, from the book, before you open the questions.
One way to do it — a mind map. Centre: No oxygen: what now?. Branches to explore:
  • The problem — What does glycolysis run out of if the mitochondria stop? Why?
  • Animals — What is pyruvate reduced to? Is CO2 released?
  • Yeast and plants — Two steps — which releases CO2, which uses reduced NAD?
  • Why bother — What does reducing pyruvate regenerate, and what does that allow?
  • Afterwards — Where does lactate go? What is it turned back into, and what does that need?
  • Yield — ATP per glucose anaerobic vs aerobic — roughly what ratio?
Whatever the format, your notes should end up containing:
  • ☐ Both pathways written as equations with 'reduced NAD → NAD' above the arrow
  • ☐ A two-column comparison table: animals vs yeast (reaction, products, reversible?)
  • ☐ A boxed sentence naming the one step unique to anaerobic respiration
  • ☐ A note on the fate of lactate

3 · Required practical 9 — rate of respiration

RP9: the effect of a named variable on the rate of respiration of cultures of single-celled organisms (yeast is standard). Two apparatus types are examined: the respirometer (June 2022 Paper 2 Q4, 9 marks across five parts) and the redox-dye / colour-endpoint method (June 2023 Paper 3 Q2). The maths of rates is where marks are lost.

Beyond the spec · Lactate is not the villain

Super-curricular: not on the specification, not scored. This is the material that turns an A-level answer into a personal-statement paragraph and an interview answer.

For most of the twentieth century lactate was taught as a waste product that causes fatigue. George Brooks (UC Berkeley) showed instead that lactate is made continuously even at rest, is shuttled from producing cells to consuming cells, and is a preferred fuel for the heart and brain — the lactate shuttle. The A-level mark scheme still wants 'lactate is oxidised back to pyruvate in the liver', which is one strand of the shuttle; a sport-science degree takes you the rest of the way.

The lactate threshold (the intensity at which blood lactate starts to accumulate faster than it is cleared) is one of the best predictors of endurance performance and the thing Nottingham's David Greenfield Human Physiology Unit measures with its rapid lactate analysers. Knowing the biochemistry behind it is what separates a sport scientist from a coach with a stopwatch.

Go and look

  • Brooks (2018) The science and translation of lactate shuttle theory — Cell Metabolism (open-access copy)
    The review that rehabilitated lactate; read the abstract and the first two pages.
  • UC Berkeley News — Rehabilitating lactate: from poison to cure (2018)
    Readable summary of Brooks's work.
  • The Real Science of Sport podcast (Ross Tucker & Mike Finch)
    Weekly, evidence-led; search the archive for lactate, threshold and fatigue.
  • Nottingham — David Greenfield Human Physiology Unit
    The lab: treadmills, cycle ergometers, lactate analysers, muscle biopsy.

Write it down

In 60–100 words: a coach tells a sprinter that 'lactic acid build-up' is why her legs burn. Correct the coach using A-level language (what is actually produced, why, what happens to it afterwards) and one sentence on what Brooks's lactate shuttle adds.
